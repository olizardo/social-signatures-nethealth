\begin{abstract}
\noindent How do individuals allocate cognitive, temporal, and emotional bandwidth across personal networks? Using passive smartphone logs and eight waves of longitudinal ego-network surveys from the \textit{NetHealth} study ($N = 491$ egos, 498,237 calls), this paper examines the structure, temporal stability, and psychological foundations of social signatures---the ranked distribution of communication effort directed to alters within discrete observation windows. Signature persistence holds across multiple timescales, with intraindividual self-divergence consistently smaller than interindividual reference divergence ($p < 0.0001$). Power-law models ($p(r) \sim r^{-\alpha}$, mean $R^2 = 0.935$) outperform exponential decay models, with decay exponents $\alpha$ stabilizing within six to eight months of continuous observation. Moving beyond call-frequency tallies, linking communication ranks to ego-network surveys ($N = 13,174$ dyads) reveals that the steep drop in interaction effort tracks relational transitions from kinship-dominated instrumental and emotional support at primary ranks to peer companionship in outer bands. Furthermore, signature stability persists despite substantial semester-to-semester turnover (averaging 80.1\%), indicating that individuals slot new entrants into pre-existing relational positions rather than restructuring their overall allocation profile. Multilevel panel models indicate that alterations in turnover and volume shifts drive temporal variation in self-divergence, while ego network clustering has a negligible effect. Finally, personality inventories reveal that Negative Emotionality strongly predicts steeper, hyper-concentrated signatures ($\beta = 0.098, p < 0.0001$), indicating that elevated negative emotionality fosters disproportionate reliance on core ties.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A central puzzle in the scientific study of human sociality is the tension between relational fluidity and structural constraint \citep{mcpherson2001birds-2dc, rivera2010dynamics-e3e}. Across the life course, individuals move through dynamic, evolving social worlds: entering new institutions, forming romantic partnerships, making new friends, and allowing older ties to lapse \citep{suitor1997its-18f}. Yet despite the continual turnover of interaction partners, personal communication behavior is not randomly distributed across contacts, nor does it restructure arbitrarily when network composition changes. Instead, relational investment is bounded by deep-seated cognitive and temporal constraints on sociality \citep{dunbar1992neocortex,dunbar1998social,miritello2013time}. While individuals can recognize hundreds of faces and maintain casual awareness of extensive social circles, active personal networks are structured into concentric, hierarchical layers: an intimate support clique of 1 to 2 confidants, a sympathy group of approximately 5 close ties, an affinity group of 12 to 15 regular contacts, and expanding outer bands of declining emotional intensity and contact frequency \citep{dunbar2018anatomy,roberts2009exploring,sutcliffe2012relationships}.

In a seminal contribution, \citet{saramaeki2014persistence} characterized this relational hierarchy using the concept of \textit{social signatures}, namely, the probability distribution of communication effort that an individual (ego) directs toward their contacts (alters), ordered from most to least frequently contacted within a given observation window. Tracking young adults making the transition from high school to university using mobile call detail records, \citet{saramaeki2014persistence} uncovered two fundamental empirical regularities: first, social signatures exhibit marked \textit{interindividual variation}, ranging from steep, hyper-concentrated profiles focused on a single alter to broad, egalitarian allocations; and second, an individual's social signature displays remarkable \textit{intraindividual persistence} over time. Crucially, this persistence held even when personal networks underwent substantial turnover, prompting \citet{saramaeki2014persistence} to hypothesize a ``slot-filling'' dynamic in which individuals maintain stable cognitive slots (e.g., for high-bandwidth, medium-bandwidth, and low-bandwidth contacts) and replenish them with new alters as old relationships fitting a specific slot fade.

Over the past decade, a rich multidisciplinary literature has expanded upon Saram{\"a}ki and colleagues' initial formulation. Scholars have investigated social signatures across diverse communication channels, including text messaging \citep{heydari2018multichannel}, online collaborative platforms and social networking sites \citep{li2018social,koltsova2021social}, and in-person sensor networks \citep{li2022evidence,adel2026preferentiality}. Physicists and network scientists have developed continuous resource allocation models \citep{tamarit2022beyond} and generative micro-foundations rooted in the competition between cumulative advantage and random exploration \citep{iniguez2023universal}, while methodological work has sought to disentangle genuine tie strength heterogeneity from network size stability \citep{heydari2024disentangling,heydari2024dynamic}. Recent inquiries have even leveraged signature distinctiveness as a behavioral biometric capable of re-identifying individuals in anonymized data \citep{jia2025multidimensional}.

Despite these theoretical, empirical, and modeling advances, fundamental open questions remain at the heart of the social signatures framework. First, there is the issue of timescale resolution as different researchers have used ad hoc criteria for observing social signatures. This leads to the question: Over what observational timescales do empirical signatures converge to stable, asymptotic parameters? Even more critically, because previous investigations have relied almost exclusively on commercial telecommunications metadata or passive digital traces, researchers have encountered a severe \textit{content black box}: commercial logs record the timing and frequency of calls or messages, but contain little information regarding dimensions of social support, emotional content, or the relational content of the underlying relationships. Does the steep drop-off between Rank 1 and lower-ranked alters correspond to content-based distinctions in social support, such as emotional comfort, instrumental advice, companionship, or financial aid? Furthermore, the behavioral mechanisms governing signature stability remain empirically unverified: does signature persistence truly reflect active ``slot-filling,'' or does it merely mirror the retention of core ties? Finally, we know little about what accounts for the observed deep interindividual variation in social signatures. What psychological dispositions and personality traits lie behind the strong patterns of interindividual variation observed producing steep versus flat signatures in the first place? 

This study addresses these questions by analyzing data from \textit{NetHealth}---a longitudinal cohort study tracking an incoming class of undergraduate students at the University of Notre Dame across two full academic years \citep{liu2018network,purta2016experiences,faust2017exploring,liu2021heterogeneous}. By pairing continuous, passive smartphone call records ($N = 491$ egos; 498,237 communication events) with an academic calendar, eight waves of ego-network surveys ($35,913$ alter nominations), complete alter-alter structural edge lists ($174,748$ ties), and longitudinal psychometric batteries (Big Five personality traits), we build a comprehensive empirical investigation of the structure, correlates, and determinants of inter- and intraindividual variation in social signatures. More specifically, we systematically evaluate signature persistence across multiple calendar and rolling timescales, test parametric power-law versus exponential scaling, establish the observation duration required for parameter convergence, ground communication ranks in multidimensional social support dimensions, test the slot-filling dynamic under top-alter turnover, and estimate the psychological and structural drivers of signature morphology.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~2 reviews the intellectual origins and recent advances in the social signatures literature, identifying five critical theoretical and empirical frontiers that our study addresses. Section~3 describes the \textit{NetHealth} dataset, temporal-window binning schemes, and the mathematical formulations of social signatures and divergence metrics. Section~4 presents the empirical baseline results on persistence, power-law scaling, and parameter convergence. Section~5 develops four substantive expansions: social support grounding for alters in different slots, the slot-filling mechanism, multilevel panel models of signature divergence, and personality determinants of signature shape. Section~6 closes by discussing the theoretical implications of our findings for evolutionary anthropology, sociology, and computational social science.

\section{Prior Work and Open Questions}

The concept of the social signature sits at the intersection of evolutionary anthropology, social network analysis, and computational network science. In this section, we review the evolution of this framework over the past decade, synthesizing findings from mobile telecommunications, online interaction platforms, generative physical models, and methodological refinements. We then articulate five foundational frontiers left open in the prior literature that the \textit{NetHealth} study is uniquely positioned to address.

\subsection{Cognitive Constraints and Persistence}

The theoretical foundation of the social signatures framework is rooted in Dunbar's influential social brain hypothesis \citep{dunbar1992neocortex,dunbar1998social,dunbar2018anatomy}, which posits that the evolution of large primate brains was driven by the cognitive demands of managing complex social relationships. Neocortical processing capacity imposes an upper constraint on the number of personal ties an individual can actively maintain, a ceiling widely recognized as Dunbar's number ($\approx 150$). Within this outer boundary, egocentric personal networks are organized into a nested hierarchy of concentric layers or circles, where group sizes scale by a factor of approximately 3: an intimate support clique of 1 to 2 primary partners, a sympathy group of roughly 5 close ties, an affinity group of 12 to 15 core friends, and broader layers of 50 and 150 acquaintances \citep{roberts2009exploring,sutcliffe2012relationships}. Crucially, maintaining social ties requires not only cognitive processing power but also the expenditure of finite temporal and emotional resources \citep{miritello2013time}. Because time is a non-renewable, zero-sum budget, individuals cannot allocate attention uniformly across their contacts; investing heavily in one relationship inevitably constrains the bandwidth available for others. 

In a foundational paper, \citet{saramaeki2014persistence} formalized this resource allocation process by introducing the concept of \textit{social signatures}. Rather than treating personal networks as unweighted graphs or simple categorical layers, Saram{\"a}ki and colleagues conceptualized an ego's relational environment as a continuous allocation profile. For an ego $i$ maintaining contacts with $K_i$ alters over an observation window $w$, alters are sorted in descending order of communication effort (measured by contact frequency or total call duration), $w_{i,(1),w} \ge w_{i,(2),w} \ge \dots \ge w_{i,(k_i),w}$. The social signature is defined as the normalized vector of interaction proportions directed to each rank $r \in \{1, \dots, k_i\}$:

\begin{equation}
    p_{i,w}(r) = \frac{w_{i,(r),w}}{\sum_{j=1}^{k_i} w_{i,(j),w}}, \quad \text{such that} \quad \sum_{r=1}^{k_i} p_{i,w}(r) = 1.
    \label{eq:signature_def}
\end{equation}

To compare signatures across time or between different individuals, \citet{saramaeki2014persistence} used the Jensen-Shannon Divergence ($\JSD$) with zero-padding on-the-fly \citep{lin1991divergence}. For two signatures $P$ and $Q$, zero-padded to common length $K = \max(|P|, |Q|)$, the distance is:
\begin{equation}
    \JSD(P \parallel Q) = H(M) - \frac{1}{2}\big[H(P) + H(Q)\big],
    \label{eq:jsd_def}
\end{equation}
where $M = \frac{1}{2}(P + Q)$ is the midpoint distribution and $H(\cdot)$ denotes the Shannon entropy in bits, $H(P) = -\sum_{r=1}^K p_r \log_2(p_r)$. 

Tracking 24 high school graduates transitioning into university over 18 months, \citet{saramaeki2014persistence} established two key empirical generalizations. First, individuals display marked \textit{interindividual} diversity in their communication profiles: while all signatures exhibit heavy-tailed skewness, some egos maintain hyper-concentrated distributions directing over 40\% of their calls to Rank 1, whereas others distribute contact effort much more evenly across top alters. Second, an individual's signature displays persistent stability over time, such that self-divergence between an ego's signatures across consecutive observation periods ($\dself$) is consistently smaller than the reference divergence between random pairs of individuals ($\dref$). 

Even more remarkably, signature persistence endures despite high turnover in network membership: between high school and university, participants replaced between 60\% and 80\% of their active contacts (Jaccard distance $1 - J \approx 0.60$--$0.80$). This striking observation led \citet{saramaeki2014persistence} to hypothesize the existence of a ``slot-filling'' mechanism: individuals do not adapt their overall allocation profile to the identities of their current alters; rather, they maintain pre-existing relational slots and dynamically slot newly acquired alters into vacated positions.

\subsection{Multichannel Communication}

Following Saram{\"a}ki and colleagues' initial findings, subsequent research examines whether social signatures are idiosyncratic to traditional voice communication via phone or reflect a universal organizing principle across contemporary digital communication channels. 

First examining commercial mobile telecommunications at large scale, \citet{heydari2018multichannel} analyzed voice call and Short Message Service (SMS) records from a European carrier spanning more than half a million egos. They confirmed that the signature persistence observed by \citet{saramaeki2014persistence} in a small student cohort held across nationwide populations. Crucially, \citet{heydari2018multichannel} showed that text messaging signatures also exhibited intraindividual persistence over time ($\dself < \dref$). Furthermore, when comparing call and SMS signatures for the same ego, they found a moderate structural resemblance, even though the specific alters contacted via voice calls and text messages showed minimal overlap. 

Broadening the inquiry to online environments, \citet{li2018social} investigated the dynamical evolution of social signatures among users of Facebook Wall posts and Wikipedia Talk pages across multiple consecutive intervals. Their empirical findings confirmed that social signatures remain stable in collaborative online networks despite continuous member churn. Extending the analytical framework, \citet{li2018social} introduced the concept of ``structural signatures'' based on topological embeddedness (the number of mutual friends shared between ego and alter in the global network). They showed that alters ranked by structural embeddedness produce rank-frequency profiles that mirror interaction-based social signatures, showing that behavioral communication intensity reflects positions within the wider community structure.

In a comprehensive multi-modal study, \citet{li2022evidence} analyzed high-resolution data from the Copenhagen Networks Study \citep{stopczynski2014measuring}, tracking more than 700 university students across three distinct interaction modes: mobile phone calls, text messages, and Bluetooth-sensed in-person physical proximity. \citet{li2022evidence} showed that social signatures were persistent across time and consistent across all three communication channels. However, they uncovered key modality-specific differences: communication networks mediated by voice calls and text messages were substantially more stable than in-person proximity networks, which exhibited higher turnover and greater susceptibility to circumstantial fluctuations. Drawing on media richness theory \citep{daft1983information}, \citet{li2022evidence} argued that individuals match the richness and synchronicity of specific communication channels to the ambiguity of relational tasks, choosing lean mediated channels for core ties while using face-to-face contact for broader social exploration.

More recently, \citet{liu2024uncovering} evaluated multi-channel mobile communications using entropy evaluation methods and null models. Their analyses revealed an empirical U-shaped trade-off between calling and texting as an individual's total contact portfolio expands, alongside clear vitality ceilings: egos maintained active communication vitality up to approximately 10 contacts in voice calling, 100 in text messaging, and 20 in hybrid communication. Together, these studies confirm that social signatures represent an enduring, cross-modal behavioral pattern, but also indicate that different communication media capture distinct slices of an individual's total relational life.

\subsection{Theoretical Models and Scaling}

In parallel with empirical extensions across platforms, theorists and statistical physicists sought to identify the mathematical laws and generative mechanisms governing social signatures. A prominent debate concerned whether personal networks are best understood as discrete, stepwise layers (the classical Dunbar circles) or as smooth, continuous distributions.

Bridging this divide, \citet{tamarit2022beyond} formulated a statistical-mechanical model of resource allocation based on the principle of maximum entropy. Rather than categorizing ties into a small number of arbitrary discrete bins, in which the cost of interaction is assumed to be uniform within each bin, Tamarit and colleagues treated relational maintenance costs as continuous variables. Their continuum model showed that discrete Dunbar circles and continuous social signatures are mathematically unified manifestations of a single resource allocation process under finite cognitive capacity. Under this formulation, they predicted and empirically identified a universal scaling parameter, $\eta \approx 6$, representing the ratio of relationship costs between adjacent levels. Evaluating datasets spanning mobile phone logs, face-to-face contacts, and Facebook interactions, \citet{tamarit2022beyond} showed that the distribution of estimated parameters across individuals clustered tightly around this universal value.

% Simultaneously, \citet{iniguez2023universal} examined the generative microfoundations of heterogeneity in tie strength. Analyzing personal networks across millions of individuals in four large-scale datasets (mobile calls, text messages, Twitter mentions, and Wikipedia talk pages), they found universal distributions of tie strengths and their individual-level variations. To explain this universality, \citet{iniguez2023universal} introduced a microscopic model of egocentric network evolution driven by a dynamic competition between two fundamental behavioral mechanisms: cumulative advantage (preferential tie reinforcement, where an ego directs communication to existing alters with probability proportional to past interactions, parameterized by an alter-preferentiality exponent $w_{ir}^\beta$) and random choice (exploratory communication, where an ego explores new ties or contacts existing alters at random, independent of past interaction history). The balance between these forces is captured by the alter-preferentiality parameter $\beta \in [0, \infty)$. When $\beta = 0$, communication is allocated uniformly across alters; as $\beta$ increases, cumulative advantage concentrates communication into a narrow elite of alters. \citet{iniguez2023universal} showed that individual variation in $\beta$ fully accounts for empirical differences in social signature steepness, proving that heavy-tailed signatures reflect a universal balancing act between exploiting core relationships and exploring peripheral connections.

% Extending this generative framework to offline physical settings, \citet{adel2026preferentiality} analyzed face-to-face wearable sensor logs and spatial colocation traces alongside online communication networks. Their findings revealed that while spatial colocation (physical proximity without active engagement) is a noisy proxy that does not exhibit the social signature structure, active face-to-face interactions conform precisely to the preferentiality dynamics identified by \citet{iniguez2023universal}. Crucially, \citet{adel2026preferentiality} showed that empirical personal networks operate under a bounded communication bandwidth, identifying a universal scaling relationship between tie preferentiality ($\beta$) and bandwidth capacity that holds across both online and offline social systems.

\subsection{Disentangling Degree and Tie Strength}

As empirical work on social signatures expanded, methodologists noted an important vulnerability in how social signature persistence had historically been measured. In a rigorous examination, \citet{heydari2024disentangling} and \citet{heydari2024dynamic} pointed out that an ego's social signature contains two distinct structural features: its length (the personal network size or degree, $k$) and the internal distribution of interaction weights across those ranks (tie strength heterogeneity).

Standard evaluations of signature persistence calculate the Jensen-Shannon Divergence or Euclidean distance between zero-padded signature vectors. However, \citet{heydari2024disentangling} showed that $\JSD$ distance is mathematically sensitive to differences in signature length: two signatures of similar length will mechanically yield a lower divergence than two signatures of disparate lengths, regardless of their internal curvature. Because an individual's network degree tends to be relatively stable across consecutive observation windows, the self-divergence ($\dself$) is systematically calculated between signatures of similar length. Conversely, the reference divergence ($\dref$) compares random pairs of individuals in the population, who often exhibit substantial differences in degree. Consequently, standard $\JSD$ comparisons risk conflating the persistence of network size with the persistence of communication allocation strategy.

To resolve this confounding, \citet{heydari2024disentangling} evaluated degree-insensitive metrics of tie strength heterogeneity, including the Gini coefficient distance:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta G(G_1, G_2) = |G_1 - G_2|, \quad \text{where} \quad G = \frac{2}{k} \frac{\sum_{r=1}^k r \cdot w_r}{\sum_{r=1}^k w_r} - \frac{k+1}{k},
    \label{eq:gini_dist}
\end{equation}
and the normalized alter-preferentiality distance derived from I{\~n}iguez and colleagues' generative model:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta \beta(\beta_1, \beta_2) = \frac{|\beta_1 - \beta_2|}{\beta_1 + \beta_2}.
    \label{eq:beta_dist}
\end{equation}
Applying these degree-invariant measures to large-scale mobile phone datasets, \citet{heydari2024disentangling} proved that the persistence of personal network structure cannot be attributed to degree stability alone. Even after strictly controlling for degree differences, intraindividual distances remained significantly smaller than interindividual reference distances. This confirmed that tie-strength heterogeneity is an authentic, degree-independent behavioral trait of egos: individuals possess an enduring personal allocation style (whether concentrated or egalitarian) that persists regardless of the number of contacts they maintain.

\subsection{Longitudinal Stability and Privacy}

Finally, two recent developments have examined the longitudinal boundaries of signature persistence and its implications for individual privacy. 

Investigating the temporal stability of social signatures over extended observation horizons, \citet{koltsova2021social} tracked private messaging on the Russian social networking site \textit{VKontakte} (VK) across 18 consecutive months. While replicating the baseline finding that short-term self-distances were smaller than interindividual reference distances ($\dself < \dref$), \citet{koltsova2021social} made a critical new discovery: intraindividual signature changes across longer spans (e.g., between Months 1--6 and Months 13--18) were statistically significant via Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests. They argued that social signatures are not immutable person-level constants; rather, they undergo subtle developmental drift as individuals experience shifting life circumstances, suggesting that signature stability is bounded by long-term life transitions.

Conversely, the very uniqueness and temporal persistence of personal allocation profiles has raised significant data privacy concerns. Showing this potential for behavioral re-identification, \citet{jia2025multidimensional} examined how multidimensional social signatures constructed from interaction timing, contact volume, and communication ranks can de-anonymize users in supposedly anonymous datasets. Using low-sensitivity interaction metadata from email, call logs, and app engagement records, \citet{jia2025multidimensional} achieved user re-identification accuracies of up to 87\%. Their findings establish that an individual's communication signature is so distinct and persistent that it operates as a behavioral biometric fingerprint, challenging conventional assumptions regarding the safety of anonymized network data under privacy regulations such as the GDPR and CCPA.

\subsection{Open Questions and Frontiers}

Despite the substantial progress achieved across these studies of the social signatures phenomenon, the literature has reached an empirical impasse defined by five major open questions that existing datasets cannot answer.

First, prior literature encounters a \textit{persistent content black box}. Commercial telecommunications records \citep{saramaeki2014persistence,heydari2018multichannel,iniguez2023universal}, online messaging logs \citep{li2018social,koltsova2021social}, and Bluetooth sensor traces \citep{li2022evidence} are completely devoid of relational content \citep{burt1985relation}. Consequently, researchers have been unable to determine what communication ranks actually mean in network theory terms: does the steep drop in communication effort between Rank 1 and lower-ranked alters reflect mere quantitative differences in contact volume, or does it mark transitions between distinct dimensions of social support \citep{wellman1990different-b11} such as emotional confiding, instrumental advice, social companionship, financial aid, or kinship versus peer friendship? While \citet{koltsova2021social} examined a subjective closeness rating on a small sample of 39 users, no study has linked large-scale communication ranks to multidimensional sociometric support taxonomies. The \textit{NetHealth} study can help address this impasse by cross-referencing $N = 13,174$ call-ranked dyads with eight waves of longitudinal ego-network surveys that record multidimensional social support and tie categories, showing that communication rank tracks sharp, content-based divisions in social support across classic Dunbar tiers.

Second, the behavioral mechanism underlying signature stability requires direct empirical verification. While \citet{saramaeki2014persistence} and \citet{heydari2024dynamic} hypothesized that signature stability persists because individuals slot new alters into pre-existing relational positions, they lacked the alter-level tracking and longitudinal survey depth required to verify this mechanism. Specifically, does an individual's signature remain stable because their core ties (Rank 1 and Rank 2) never change, or does stability truly endure when an individual experiences complete turnover of their primary confidant? Leveraging continuous passive phone logs across eight academic semesters with an average semester alter turnover of 80.1\%, we explicitly separate transitions where an ego retains their primary alter from transitions where the primary alter is completely replaced. We show that even under complete turnover of the top confidant, signature divergence shifts only marginally (from 0.043 to 0.078), providing direct empirical confirmation of the slot-filling hypothesis.

Third, the psychological foundations of signature curvature remain unmapped. Theoretical models of personal network evolution \citep{dunbar2018anatomy,iniguez2023universal,adel2026preferentiality} assume that individual differences in alter-preferentiality ($\beta$) and signature decay exponents ($\alpha$) reflect internal cognitive and affective dispositions. Yet commercial telecommunications providers have no psychometric data on their subscribers, and university sensor studies \citep{li2022evidence} did not model personality as a predictor of signature steepness. Beyond preliminary exploratory correlations \citep{centellegher2017personality}, no study has rigorously estimated the extent to which validated personality dimensions govern the curvature of social signatures. We link objective communication decay exponents ($\alpha_i$) to longitudinal psychometric batteries measuring the Big Five personality traits. Through regression modeling, we show that Negative Emotionality strongly predicts steeper, hyper-concentrated signatures ($\beta = 0.098, p < 0.0001$), revealing that heightened negative emotionality drives disproportionate reliance on a narrow core of alters.

Fourth, the field lacks empirical guidance on multi-timescale hierarchies and parameter burn-in convergence. Prior empirical investigations evaluated social signatures at arbitrary, isolated temporal resolutions: \citet{li2022evidence} examined 1-month intervals, \citet{heydari2018multichannel} used 6-month windows, and \citet{saramaeki2014persistence} examined 9-month terms. However, no study has evaluated how signature stability behaves across a nested hierarchy of timescales within the same population. More crucially, prior literature offers no guidance on parameter burn-in: how long must an individual be continuously observed before their estimated signature decay parameter $\alpha$ converges to its true asymptotic value? We evaluate social signatures across eight temporal window schemes spanning calendar definitions (academic years, semesters, quarters, months) and rolling weekly intervals (3-week rolling, 2-week discrete, 1-week discrete). Furthermore, by tracking cumulative observation intervals from 2 to 24 months, we establish an empirical burn-in threshold showing that personal signature parameters stabilize within 6 to 8 months of continuous observation.

Finally, personal communication signatures must be situated within their broader network structural embedding. Previous studies have modeled communication signatures in isolation from the structural connectivity of the surrounding network. Yet communication is embedded within broader community structures, residence halls, and friendship groups \citep{li2018social}. How do alter turnover, ego network transitivity (clustering), and shifts in overall communication volume jointly govern signature stability over time? \textit{NetHealth} pairs continuous individual phone logs with 174,748 alter-alter structural ties connecting peers within each respondent's personal egocentric network across survey waves. We estimate multilevel linear mixed-effects models predicting signature self-divergence ($\dself$) across consecutive semesters as a joint function of dyadic Jaccard turnover, triadic clustering, and communication volume shifts, providing the first structural panel evaluation of signature self-divergence.

\section{Data and Methods}

\subsection{The NetHealth Cohort}
The \textit{NetHealth} study recruited an entire incoming cohort of first-year undergraduate students at the University of Notre Dame beginning in Fall 2015 \citep{liu2018network,purta2016experiences,faust2017exploring}; comprehensive study design, instrumentation, and protocols are documented on the project website at \url{https://sites.nd.edu/nethealth/}. Participants consented to continuous digital sensing via their smartphones and Fitbit devices, alongside dense ego-network and psychological surveys administered at regular intervals throughout their college careers. 

Our analytical design links five primary data streams. First, continuous communication logs capture 498,237 outgoing voice call events collected from participant iOS devices spanning June 30, 2015, through July 2, 2017, across 491 unique egos. Second, comprehensive institutional academic calendar records document semester start and end dates, examination periods, orientation, and vacation breaks. Third, eight longitudinal waves of ego-network surveys record 35,913 alter nominations, capturing tie categories (family kin versus peer friends), perceived closeness, interpersonal trust, and multidimensional social support. Fourth, complete alter-alter edge lists document 174,748 structural ties connecting peers within each respondent's personal egocentric network across survey waves. Finally, longitudinal psychometric survey batteries measure the Big Five personality dimensions (Extraversion, Agreeableness, Conscientiousness, Negative Emotionality [historically termed Neuroticism], Openness).

\subsection{Temporal Window Definitions}
To establish whether social signatures persist across differing temporal resolutions, we construct two complementary binning schemes. The first scheme relies on calendar-based windows spanning July 1, 2015 through June 30, 2017. This includes two academic years (July 2015--June 2016 and July 2016--June 2017), four collegiate semesters (Fall 2015, Spring 2016, Fall 2016, and Spring 2017), eight academic quarters (July--September, October--December, January--March, and April--June), and 24 consecutive calendar months. The second scheme evaluates week-based windows spanning July 6, 2015 through July 2, 2017 across 104 Monday-to-Sunday weeks. This includes 102 three-week rolling windows evaluated in one-week increments, 52 discrete two-week intervals, and 104 discrete one-week intervals.

To ensure that estimated signatures reflect meaningful personal communication structures, egos are required to meet two standard inclusion criteria: (a) a minimum of 2 active alters in every window ($k_{i,w} \ge 2$), and (b) an average call volume exceeding 10 calls per window. As summarized in Table~\ref{tab:cohort_summary}, while the overarching sensing pool comprises $N = 491$ unique egos with outgoing voice calls, the number of eligible participants naturally varies across timescales depending on the number of consecutive observation intervals required. The broadest calendar resolution (academic years, $W = 2$) retains $N = 388$ eligible egos. The collegiate semester timescale ($W = 4$) retains $N = 290$ participants, serving as our primary analytical sample for all subsequent dyadic, structural, and psychological modeling. Monthly and rolling weekly windows impose stricter longitudinal compliance across 24 to 104 intervals, retaining $N = 147$ and $N = 88$ egos, respectively. When directly benchmarking stability metrics across calendar resolutions, we evaluate the common calendar cohort ($N = 147$ egos) present in all four schemes simultaneously.

\subsection{Signatures and Divergence Metrics}
For ego $i$ in temporal window $w$, communication weights $w_{i,j,w}$ (measured as total outgoing voice calls) to alters $j = 1, \dots, k_{i,w}$ are sorted in descending order:
\begin{equation}
w_{i,(1),w} \ge w_{i,(2),w} \ge \dots \ge w_{i,(k),w}
\end{equation}
The social signature is defined as the normalized vector of interaction proportions:
\begin{equation}
p_{i,w}(r) = \frac{w_{i,(r),w}}{W_{i,w}}, \quad \text{where } W_{i,w} = \sum_{j=1}^{k_{i,w}} w_{i,j,w} \quad \text{and} \quad \sum_{r=1}^{k_{i,w}} p_{i,w}(r) = 1
\end{equation}
where $r \in \{1, 2, \dots, k_{i,w}\}$ represents the alter rank.

To quantify the similarity or difference between two probability vectors $P = \{p_r\}_{r=1}^{k_1}$ and $Q = \{q_r\}_{r=1}^{k_2}$ of potentially unequal lengths $k_1 \neq k_2$, we append zeros to the shorter distribution up to length $K = \max(k_1, k_2)$ on-the-fly and compute the \textit{Jensen-Shannon Divergence} \citep[JSD;][]{lin1991divergence}:
\begin{equation}
\JSD(P \parallel Q) = H(M) - \frac{1}{2}\left[H(P) + H(Q)\right]
\end{equation}
where $M = \frac{1}{2}(P + Q)$ is the mixture distribution, and $H(P)$ denotes Shannon entropy measured in bits:
\begin{equation}
H(P) = -\sum_{r=1}^{K} p_r \log_2(p_r) \quad (\text{with } 0 \log_2 0 \equiv 0)
\end{equation}
JSD is symmetric ($\JSD(P \parallel Q) = \JSD(Q \parallel P)$), non-negative, and strictly bounded between 0 (identical distributions) and 1 bit (disjoint distributions).

For evaluating consistency across multiple observation windows simultaneously, we also compute the generalized $n$-ary Jensen-Shannon divergence across a set of $n$ distributions $\{P_1, P_2, \dots, P_n\}$:
\begin{equation}
\JSD(P_1, \dots, P_n) = H\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{m=1}^n P_m\right) - \frac{1}{n}\sum_{m=1}^n H(P_m)
\end{equation}

We evaluate stability by defining two core divergence metrics. First, self-divergence ($\dself$) measures the mean pairwise JSD between consecutive temporal windows for the same ego across observation periods:
\begin{equation}
\dself(i) = \frac{1}{W-1} \sum_{w=1}^{W-1} \JSD(P_{i,w} \parallel P_{i,w+1}).
\end{equation}
Second, reference-divergence ($\dref$) quantifies the divergence between the social signatures of distinct individuals observed within the same temporal window. This can be evaluated at the level of individual egos or across all unique participant pairs:
\begin{align}
\text{By Ego: } \dref(i) &= \frac{1}{W} \sum_{w=1}^W \left[ \frac{1}{n_w - 1} \sum_{j \neq i} \JSD(P_{i,w} \parallel P_{j,w}) \right], \\
\text{All Pairs: } \bar{d}_{\mathrm{ref}} &= \frac{1}{\binom{n}{2}} \sum_{i < j} \left[ \frac{1}{W} \sum_{w=1}^W \JSD(P_{i,w} \parallel P_{j,w}) \right].
\end{align}

If personal social signatures exhibit persistent idiosyncratic stability over time, an individual's self-divergence must be systematically and significantly smaller than the reference divergence observed between random peers ($\dself < \dref$).

\subsection{Evaluative and Cognitive Alignment: Closeness, Duration, and Salience}

Beyond discrete relational content divisions based on role relations or social support, an alter's position slot in the social signature is strongly and systematically correlated with primary evaluative and cognitive dimensions of the social tie (Figure~\ref{fig:tie_evaluative} and Table~\ref{tab:tie_regressions}). 

First, subjective closeness \citep{marsden1984measuring-f62} exhibits a steep monotonic gradient across signature tiers ($F = 280.1, p < 0.0001$): 93.7\% of alters occupying the Rank 1 slot are rated as ``especially close'' (mean rating 3.93 on a 1--4 scale), declining steadily to 78.5\% at Ranks 4--5, and 52.7\% at Ranks $>20$ ($r = -0.222, \rho = -0.310$; with call proportion $r = +0.238, p < 0.0001$; Figure~\ref{fig:tie_evaluative}A). Second, tie duration, or relationship age \citep{burt2000decay-f08} displays an even more pronounced structural sorting ($F = 581.0, p < 0.0001$): Rank 1 alters have been known for an average of 14.2 years (median 18.8 years), capturing lifelong kin and long-term relationships, whereas alters in intermediate tiers (Ranks 6--10) average 7.0 years (median 3.0 years) and outer tiers average 4.9 years (median 2.1 years), representing ties formed upon collegiate matriculation ($r = -0.266, \rho = -0.355$; with call proportion $r = +0.333, p < 0.0001$; Figure~\ref{fig:tie_evaluative}B). Third, social signature position corresponds closely to cognitive salience, operationalized as the alter's recall order during survey elicitation ($F = 334.0, p < 0.0001$): 36.4\% of Rank 1 alters are recalled and named first in the survey (71.2\% within the top 5), whereas alters in outer tiers average a recall position of 9.9 ($r = +0.243, \rho = +0.345$; with call proportion $r = +0.282, p < 0.0001$; Figure~\ref{fig:tie_evaluative}C).

\subsection{The Slot-Filling Mechanism Under Alter Turnover}
A central theoretical puzzle in social signatures research is how an ego's signature can remain stable despite the reality of frequent alter replacement. To investigate how frequent alter replacement is in the \textit{NetHealth} data, we calculated the dyadic Jaccard turnover between consecutive semesters:

\begin{equation}
\Turnover_{i,w} = 1 - \frac{|A_{i,w} \cap A_{i,w+1}|}{|A_{i,w} \cup A_{i,w+1}|}
\end{equation}

where $A_{i,w}$ denotes the set of active alters for ego $i$ in window $w$.

Remarkably, \textit{alter turnover between consecutive semesters averages 80.1\% ($\text{SD} = 0.076$)}. Despite replacing four out of five alters every six months, egos maintain an average self-divergence of only 0.0614. In 59.1\% of consecutive semester intervals, the single top-ranked alter was retained; even when the top alter was replaced, the overall self-divergence increased only modestly (from 0.0431 to 0.0785, Figure~\ref{fig:turnover}). 

This pattern provides direct empirical confirmation of the \textit{slot-filling hypothesis}: an individual maintains a predefined cognitive template for relationship allocation, seamlessly slotting new social entrants into vacant relational positions without perturbing their structural allocation curve.

While linear regression provides an omnibus test of parametric associations, it assumes additive relationships that may overlook non-linear thresholds or complex psychological interactions. To uncover the hierarchical structure governing attention concentration, we trained a regression tree (Classification and Regression Tree; CART; \citealt{breiman1984classification}) predicting mean power-law decay exponents ($\alpha_i$) from baseline Big Five traits (Figure~\ref{fig:cart_tree}). 

The CART model confirms the primacy of Negative Emotionality while uncovering a critical moderating role for Extraversion. Variable importance diagnostics assign 49\% of relative predictive power to Negative Emotionality, followed by Extraversion (19\%), Openness (16\%), Agreeableness (13\%), and Conscientiousness (2\%). The primary root split bifurcates the cohort on Negative Emotionality at a cutoff of 2.44 (distinguishing the bottom tertile from the rest of the sample). Participants with low Negative Emotionality ($< 2.44$, $n = 98$) form an immediate terminal leaf characterized by comparatively flat, egalitarian attention distributions ($\bar{\alpha} = 1.12$).

Among individuals in the top two tertiles of Negative Emotionality ($\ge 2.44$, $n = 192$), the tree splits conditionally on Extraversion at a threshold of 2.56. Introverted individuals with high Negative Emotionality (Extraversion $< 2.56$, $n = 40$) exhibit relatively high relational concentration ($\bar{\alpha} = 1.32$). Within this subset, those who are also high in Agreeableness ($\ge 3.61$, $n = 20$) direct communication almost exclusively into one confidant, producing the highest observed decay exponents ($\bar{\alpha} = 1.39$). Conversely, moderate-to-high Extraversion ($\ge 2.56$, $n = 152$) acts as a psychological buffer against hyper-concentration ($\bar{\alpha} = 1.22$), keeping attention more evenly distributed across contacts unless Negative Emotionality reaches severe levels ($\ge 3.06$, $\bar{\alpha} = 1.27$--$1.33$). This non-linear buffering dynamic clarifies why Extraversion showed no linear main effect in the linear model, as it operates \textit{conditionally} as a protective moderator among individuals with high negative emotionality rather than as an independent main effect.

\section{Discussion}

By linking continuous passive smartphone logs with multi-wave ego-network surveys and psychological batteries in the NetHealth cohort, this study provides empirical backing for social signature theory while advancing the paradigm into social network analysis and computational psychology. The findings resolve long-standing theoretical puzzles regarding the interplay between relational fluidity and cognitive constraint across life transitions.

\subsection{Summary of Key Results}

In this paper, we have shown that social signatures are an individual-level regularity that holds across multiple observational timescales. Across all eight temporal window definitions, intraindividual self-divergence ($\dself$) is systematically and significantly smaller than interindividual reference divergence ($\dref$, $p < 0.0001$ across all resolutions, with Cohen's $d > 1.4$). In collegiate semester windows, mean self-divergence is 0.0614, less than half the reference divergence observed between random peers ($\dref = 0.1184$). This stability persists not only at coarse-grained annual scales ($\dself = 0.0633$ versus $\dref = 0.1129$) but also across fine-grained weekly intervals ($\dself = 0.0495$ versus $\dref = 0.1411$ in three-week rolling windows). Formal nonparametric location tests confirm that personal communication profiles reflect enduring behavioral traits rather than stochastic fluctuations.

Second, our parametric evaluations clarify the mathematical morphology of social signatures. Power-law decay models ($p(r) = c \cdot r^{-\alpha}$, mean $R^2 = 0.935$) decisively outperform exponential decay specifications ($R^2 = 0.746$), with the power-law model preferred by Akaike's Information Criterion in 97.0\% of semester specifications. The estimated power-law exponent averages $\alpha = 1.22$ ($\text{SD} = 0.25$), capturing a heavy-tailed allocation profile where the single top-ranked alter commands 35\% to 45\% of total communication bandwidth, while lower ranks taper into a prolonged tail of infrequent contacts. Crucially, longitudinal parameter burn-in tracking across 24 consecutive months shows that an ego's estimated decay exponent converges to its asymptotic value within six to eight months of continuous digital observation (mean absolute error $< 0.10$). This establishes an empirical benchmark for the observation duration in subsequent social signatures research.

Third, our analysis helps open the content black box in previous work by linking 13,174 call-ranked dyads to longitudinal ego-network survey nominations across six social signature Dunbar tiers. We show that communication ranks are not arbitrary artifacts of call logging; rather, they delineate sharp boundaries in relational contents, such as role relations, social support, subjective closeness, tie duration and cognitive salience. The top-ranked alter constitutes a specialized kinship-dominated support hub (69.7\% family kin, primarily parents and romantic partners), providing the structural backbone of emotional support (85.4\%), instrumental advice (87.6\%), and financial assistance (64.1\%). Ranks 2 and 3 represent the core sympathy group, retaining high kinship density (62.2\%) and emotional support (77.1\%). Between Ranks 3 and 5, personal networks undergo a structural transition toward dominance of peer friendships (56.9\% of friends at Ranks 4--5, rising to 87.8\% beyond Rank 20). Notably, while emotional and advisory support decline monotonically across ranks, companionship remains elevated across all tiers (averaging over 69\% even for outer ties), indicating that peripheral communication serves essential social, leisure, and recreational integration functions.

Fourth, linking communication ranks to survey evaluative measures reveals that social signature position slots strongly correspond to relationship longevity, cognitive accessibility, and emotional closeness. In linear mixed-effects models accounting for the nesting of alters within egos ($N = 10,828$ dyads across 280 individuals, $\text{ICC} = 0.109$), relationship duration ($t = 30.26, p < 0.0001$), cognitive recall salience ($t = 23.47, p < 0.0001$), interpersonal trust ($t = 6.95, p < 0.0001$), and subjective closeness ($t = 4.73, p < 0.0001$) each exert strong, independent positive effects on communication allocation. Over 93\% of alters in the primary slot are evaluated as especially close and average 14.2 years of relational duration (median 18.8 years), compared to 52.7\% and 4.9 years for outer ties. Furthermore, 71.2\% of primary alters are recalled within the first five alters elicited in survey name generators (36.4\% named first), confirming that behavioral communication ranks reflect top-of-mind cognitive retrieval priority alongside accumulated relational history.

Fifth, we provide direct empirical verification of the slot-filling mechanism. Despite an average semester-to-semester alter turnover of 80.1\%, egos preserve signature stability ($\dself = 0.0614$). In 59.1\% of consecutive intervals, individuals retain their top-ranked alter; even when the primary confidant is completely replaced by a new entrant, self-divergence increases only marginally from 0.0431 to 0.0785. This establishes that individuals do not reorganize their allocation curve around specific social partners; rather, they maintain pre-existing relational slots and dynamically slot newly acquired alters into vacated functional positions.

Sixth, our structural panel models illuminate the systemic drivers of signature stability over time. Multilevel linear mixed-effects panel models show that alter turnover ($\beta = 0.351, p < 0.0001$) and percentage activity shifts ($\beta = 0.0014, p < 0.0001$) are the primary drivers of temporal variation in signature self-divergence, whereas local ego network clustering has no significant effect net of turnover. This indicates that signature stability is governed by individual-level relational habits rather than the triadic closure of the surrounding peer group.

Finally, econometric regression and decision-tree modeling reveal that social signature steepness is anchored in psychological dispositions, particularly Negative Emotionality. In parametric linear regressions, Negative Emotionality strongly predicts steeper, core-concentrated allocation profiles ($\beta = 0.098, p < 0.0001$), with students in the highest Negative Emotionality group directing 31.7\% of all outgoing communication to a single primary alter, compared to 25.3\% for peers low in Negative Emotionality. Going beyond linear assumptions, a classification and regression tree (CART) establishes that Negative Emotionality forms the primary root partition ($< 2.44$ vs.\ $\ge 2.44$, accounting for 49\% of relative variable importance), producing an egalitarian terminal leaf ($\bar{\alpha} = 1.121$) for low scorers. Crucially, the decision tree uncovers a non-linear moderating role for Extraversion among individuals with elevated Negative Emotionality: introversion ($< 2.56$) exacerbates relational withdrawal and hyper-concentration ($\bar{\alpha} = 1.322$, and up to $1.392$ when combined with high Agreeableness), whereas moderate-to-high Extraversion ($\ge 2.56$) acts as a protective buffer ($\bar{\alpha} = 1.223$). This non-linear buffering dynamic explains why Extraversion showed no linear main effect in standard regressions, establishing that individual allocation strategy reflects complex, interactive personality profiles.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}

While this study offers an empirical synthesis of social signature dynamics, several methodological and theoretical limitations suggest promising directions for future research. We address five essential analytical dimensions in sequence: operationalization and measurement constraints, threats to causal inference, directionality and selection feedback loops, temporal granularity and panel attrition, and institutional scope conditions.

Regarding operationalization and measurement constraints, our communication logs are derived exclusively from passive records of outgoing voice calls on participants' smartphones. While voice calls represent a high-investment communication channel characterized by synchronized verbal interaction, contemporary personal communication is distributed across multiple mediated channels, including text messaging (SMS), instant messaging platforms, social media direct messages, and video calls. Prior multichannel studies show that while social signatures persist within voice and text channels independently, alter composition across channels exhibits limited overlap. Although our voice records capture the high-bandwidth core of personal support networks, future research should integrate multi-stream digital sensing to evaluate whether composite multichannel signatures exhibit greater structural stability or channel-specific functional specialization.

Threats to causal inference and contextual confounding also warrant careful consideration. In residential university environments, social interactions are shaped by exogenous organizational structures, including freshman residence hall assignments, academic major tracks, dining hall layouts, and classroom schedules. While our study models individual-level dispositions and ego network metrics, unmeasured architectural and institutional opportunity structures could constrain or facilitate interaction opportunities independent of an individual's intrinsic relational preferences. Future work should pair passive sensing with spatial positioning and administrative records to disentangle voluntary relationship maintenance from environmentally induced contact.

A related challenge concerns directionality, selection, and feedback loops. While our models establish that baseline Negative Emotionality predicts steeper signature concentration, the causal interplay between psychological states and communication structure may be bidirectional. An individual high in negative emotionality may actively retreat into an exclusive relationship to manage anxiety; conversely, becoming structurally dependent on an exclusive social tie may exacerbate negative emotionality and hinder broader socialization. Future research should implement continuous-time structural equation models or cross-lagged panel models to evaluate how fluctuations in signature steepness co-evolve with psychological distress and emotional states over time.

Issues of temporal granularity and panel attrition present additional analytical considerations. Our calendar windows follow institutional collegiate semesters, capturing the natural rhythms of undergraduate academic life. However, semester windows aggregate interaction dynamics across six-month periods, potentially smoothing over rapid, micro-temporal churn that occurs during intense examination periods, athletic seasons, or vacation breaks. Combining continuous passive logging with high-frequency ecological momentary assessment (EMA) could capture how social signatures adapt to short-term stressors. Furthermore, while the NetHealth study maintains high longitudinal compliance, panel attrition over multi-year undergraduate studies requires ongoing attention to potential survival bias among highly compliant participants.

Finally, the institutional scope conditions of this study must be underscored. Our analytic sample consists of undergraduate students at an elite, residential, private American university (the University of Notre Dame). Young adulthood represents a unique developmental phase characterized by geographic relocation, high relational mobility, identity formation, and intensive peer exploration. While this transitional context provides an ideal testing ground for signature stability under alter turnover, the empirical parameters observed here may not generalize directly to working adults, older populations, or non-residential community settings where relational turnover is substantially lower and family obligations are more institutionalized. Comparative cross-national and cross-institutional studies across diverse demographic strata will be essential to establish the global boundaries of social signature morphology.

\subsection{Implications: Cognitive Templates and Capital}

These empirical discoveries carry significant theoretical implications for social network analysis, evolutionary anthropology, and computational network science. For decades, network theories of social capital have emphasized the strategic advantages of network expansion, weak ties, and structural brokerage \citep{burt1992structural,granovetter1973strength}. Under these classical formulations, social actors are portrayed as flexible investors who build, expand, and reshape their relational portfolios to maximize instrumental returns. Conversely, evolutionary anthropology has emphasized biological and cognitive constraints, positing that human neocortical capacity strictly bounds the volume of relationships an individual can track \citep{dunbar1998social,dunbar2018anatomy}.

Our findings transcend this zero-sum theoretical dichotomy by showing how structural flexibility and cognitive constraint coexist via persistent allocation templates. Individuals do not manage networks on an unconstrained, dyad-by-dyad basis; rather, they deploy an enduring cognitive template that allocates relational investment across a structured hierarchy of roles. When social turnover occurs, individuals preserve their structural signature by seamlessly slotting new social entrants into pre-existing functional positions. The slot-filling dynamic reveals that personal networks possess a homeostatic structural equilibrium: the specific alters occupying the network are fluid and disposable, but the relational geometry governing their attention is rigid and enduring.

Furthermore, our integration of ego-network survey evaluations with continuous digital communication logs clarifies the micro-foundations of tie strength. Following classical formulations \citep{granovetter1973strength,marsden1984measuring-f62,burt2000decay-f08}, tie strength has long been conceptualized as a multidimensional construct encompassing emotional intensity, intimacy, reciprocal support, and longevity. Our findings show that social signatures reflect the behavioral enactment of this multidimensionality: an alter's position slot in the signature is determined not merely by situational communication opportunities, but by the joint confluence of relationship longevity (accumulated biographical capital), cognitive recall salience (top-of-mind mental accessibility), and subjective intimacy. High-bandwidth communication serves as the ongoing behavioral mechanism that sustains this core, while lower-bandwidth peripheral ties provide flexible avenues for companionship and social exploration.

Finally, uncovering the psychological foundations of signature curvature—both through parametric regressions and non-linear decision trees—bridges the gap between individual personality dispositions and network morphology. Social signature steepness is not an arbitrary structural quirk; rather, it reflects psychologically grounded relational strategies. The discovery that Negative Emotionality drives core concentration indicates that individuals prone to anxiety and emotional vulnerability adopt a defensive, risk-averse allocation strategy, focusing their scarce emotional resources on an exclusive, highly trusted confidant. Importantly, our decision-tree results reveal that this inward orientation is conditionally moderated by Extraversion: outgoing sociability acts as a counter-balancing buffer that preserves broader social connectivity even under heightened emotional reactivity, whereas introversion accelerates retreat into an exclusive relational dyad. In clinical and applied settings, tracking personal social signatures through passive digital sensing offers a promising, non-invasive method for identifying disruptions in relational equilibrium, providing early-warning indicators for social withdrawal and psychological distress during major life-course transitions.
